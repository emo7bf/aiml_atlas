The logQ Boutique · Sampled Softmax, Explained Slowly

Alice has clicked three dresses.
The catalog has a billion. Now what?

We'll unfold sampled softmax, in-batch negatives, the mysterious logQ correction, and contrastive learning — line by line, with a toy fashion store and a 3D embedding animation you can spin. The RecSys deck named these things; today we'll mean them. As a bonus: why ScaNN beats using PCA alone.

Audience note: she knows two-tower models exist; what's missing is the derivation of logQ (importance sampling in disguise) and the pull-push mental model of contrastive learning. Build the intuition before the equations.
The Scene · A Boutique at 10⁹

Alice's user vector u. Every dress is a vector v. Relevance = their dot product u · v.

Both towers map into the same space (say, 64-d). Recommendation becomes one question: for Alice's vector u, which dress vectors v have the biggest dot product u·v?
Alice's positives (dresses she clicked) → want u · v⁺ to be high.
Everything else → want their dot products lower.
That's the signal. The only drama: how do you say "everything else" when "everything else" has 10⁹ items?
Fashion example chosen because clusters are intuitive — boho, cocktail, athletic, work. Users click within clusters: Alice likes boho, Bob likes athletic. The model must learn those clusters from clicks alone.
Step 1 · The Honest (Impossible) Loss

Full softmax: treat picking the right dress as a 10⁹-way classification.

If compute were free, we'd minimize this for each click of Alice's:
L  =  −log   exp(u · v⁺) Σk ∈ V exp(u · vk)
u · v⁺dot product of Alice with her positive dress — want this big
exp(…)turn a score into a positive number (softmax ingredient)
Vthe vocabulary — EVERY dress in the catalog (all 10⁹)
Σ … exp(u·vk)the normalizer (also called "the partition function Z") — sums over the whole catalog
−logturns probability into loss: higher probability of the positive ⇒ lower loss
Beautifully correct. Also physically impossible. 10⁹ dot products × every click × every step. We need a way to fake that denominator well.
Have her stare at the denominator. The ENTIRE difficulty of training retrieval models is "the normalizer is impossible." Everything that follows (sampled softmax, NCE, InfoNCE, in-batch, logQ) is a different way to approximate it.
Step 2 · Sampled Softmax — Approximate the Sum

Draw a tiny sample S of items. Pretend the catalog is just S.

Instead of summing 10⁹ terms, sum over a sample S of maybe 1000:
L̂  =  −log   exp(u · v⁺) exp(u · v⁺)  +  Σk ∈ S exp(u · vk)

The denominator is now: the positive + the sample of negatives. Cheap. One nice problem solved. One subtle problem invited.

Two new problems, meet them one at a time:
• Problem A: uniform sampling wastes you on obvious negatives (sports gear when Alice is shopping dresses).
• Problem B: any non-uniform sampling makes L̂ a biased estimator of L. You'd be optimizing the wrong thing. ← this is what logQ fixes.
The common confusion: students think "sampling" means "it still works, just approximately." It doesn't — biased sampling leads to systematically wrong gradients. Importance sampling / logQ is what rescues the math.
Step 3 · In-Batch Negatives — The Free Trick

If you're training on a batch of 512 (user, dress) pairs anyway, you already have 511 free negatives per row.

Your batch is a stack of 512 (user, clicked-dress) pairs. For Alice at row 1, the clicked dresses in rows 2 … 512 are almost certainly not hers → treat them as her negatives. Their embeddings? Already computed. Compute cost: zero.
But Q(dress) — the probability a dress appears in the batch as "someone's positive" (and so as everyone else's negative) — is proportional to its popularity, not uniform.

Popular dresses: appear as a negative constantly. Rare dresses: barely at all. The model, if left alone, will learn to systematically under-rank popular items — because it keeps getting punished for scoring them highly when they were someone else's positive, not Alice's.
This is the "popularity skew" in the slide you asked about. Next slide: how logQ exactly undoes it.
The popularity-skew intuition is key. Alice likes boho florals. The LBD (little black dress) is popular overall — appears as 50 users' positive in a batch of 100 → shows up as a negative for 50+ other users every batch. Without correction, the model learns "LBD is bad for everyone" — wrong!
Step 4 · logQ, Line by Line

Importance sampling, wearing a disguise.

We want the full-catalog sum. We have a sampled sum. Importance sampling turns one into an unbiased estimate of the other by dividing each term by its sampling probability. Walk with me:
# what we want:    Z = Σk ∈ V exp(u · vk)    (impossible)
# importance-sampled estimator:    Ẑ = (1/n) · Σk ∈ S   exp(u · vk) Q(k)
# equivalent (push Q into the exponent):    Ẑ = (1/n) · Σk ∈ S exp( u · vk − log Q(k) )

And there's logQ. It's the same math as importance sampling, moved into the exponent.

Mathematically: exp(a)/Q = exp(a) · exp(-log Q) = exp(a - log Q). The correction term sits in the exponent so the whole thing still looks like a softmax. The sampled softmax with logQ correction is an unbiased estimator of the full softmax.
Step 4 (cont'd) · logQ with Real Numbers

Two dresses, same score, different popularity. Watch what logQ does.

Dressraw score u·vpopularity Q−log Qcorrected score
👗 LBD (super popular)2.00.5+0.692.69
🌸 Rare boho piece2.00.005+5.307.30

Both had the same raw dot product. But the rare boho piece appears in the sample 100× less often than the LBD — so when it DOES appear in the denominator, it must stand in for the hundred other unsampled rare dresses with similar scores. logQ amplifies its weight by exactly the right amount to make this stand-in honest.

One sentence: adding −log Q(k) to every item's score makes the sampled softmax give, in expectation, the same gradient as the impossible full-catalog softmax. Tiny line of code, correctness preserved at planetary scale.
Google ships this in TensorFlow Recommenders as `tfrs.tasks.Retrieval(remove_accidental_hits=True, sampling_probability=...)`. The "senior tell" is remembering to pass in Q (or use sampled_softmax_loss with sampled_values) — forgetting silently biases training. Cite Yi et al., "Sampling-Bias-Corrected Neural Modeling for Large Corpus Item Recommendations" (Google, 2019).
Step 5 · The Same Thing, Called Contrastive Learning

Pull positives close. Push negatives away. Simultaneously, in one loss.

Contrastive learning is a reframe: the softmax loss as a physics simulation.
L  =  −log   exp(u · v⁺ / τ) Σk exp(u · vk / τ)     ( ← "InfoNCE loss")

① Minimize −log(numerator) ⇒ make u · v⁺ big. The positive gets pulled toward Alice.
② Minimize log(denominator) ⇒ make every u · vk small. Every negative gets pushed away from Alice.
③ τ (temperature): small τ → sharp (really commit to the top); large τ → diffuse (gentler gradients).

Same equation powers CLIP (images ↔ text), SimCLR (self-supervised vision), two-tower retrieval (users ↔ items). "Sampled softmax" and "contrastive learning" and "InfoNCE" and "CLIP loss" are all names for this one line.
CLIP's one-sentence summary: train an image encoder and a text encoder so matching (image, caption) pairs dot-product high and non-matching pairs dot-product low — on a batch of 32k. Exact same recipe as two-tower, with modalities instead of users+items.

Your Side-Question · Why ScaNN and Not Just PCA?

They're not competitors. They solve different problems.

PCA · a dimensionality-reduction tool

Input: a matrix of vectors. Output: a smaller matrix of vectors (fewer dims), via linear projection onto the directions of largest variance.

What it preserves: ℓ₂ distances (approximately). What it's for: visualization (768-d → 3-d), compression, noise reduction, feature engineering.

What it is NOT: a search algorithm. After PCA you still need to scan every dress to find Alice's top-10.

ScaNN · an approximate nearest-neighbor search index

Input: a billion vectors + a query vector. Output: the top-k by inner product, in milliseconds.

What it preserves: the ranking of inner products — which dress is best, not the exact score. What it's for: serving.

Its special trick — anisotropic quantization: when ScaNN compresses each dress vector, it allows error perpendicular to typical query vectors (perpendicular error doesn't change the dot product) but penalizes error parallel to them (parallel error changes rankings). PCA has no such query-awareness — every direction is equal to it.

The right picture: you can even use PCA inside ScaNN (compress 768-d → 128-d with PCA, then build a ScaNN index on top). But PCA alone makes retrieval smaller, not faster. ScaNN makes it fast by not comparing against every dress at all — it partitions the embedding space into regions, searches only the relevant partitions, and inside each partition uses its ranking-aware quantization.

The one-sentence answer: PCA compresses, ScaNN searches. Anisotropic quantization (Guo et al. 2020, Google) is the headline algorithmic win: inner-product-aware loss function for the quantization step. PCA minimizes ℓ₂ reconstruction error, which can be arbitrarily bad for inner-product ranking if the lost variance happens to align with query directions.
Take This With You

The arc: impossible softmax → sample it → batch the samples for free → subtract log Q to make the sampling honest → the same equation is called contrastive learning / InfoNCE / CLIP, where it reads as "pull positives, push negatives."

And when you need to serve the result, you reach for ScaNN — not because PCA is bad, but because PCA compresses while ScaNN searches, and only one of those is a retrieval system.
