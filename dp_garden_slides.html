🌿 The DP Garden · Guided Reps

Six reps. Each one: a question, a breath,
then the answer builds itself.

Rules of the garden: every Q slide poses a problem — actually try it (out loud, like the real room) before advancing. The A slide then draws the table, types the code the way you'd narrate it to an interviewer, and names the instinct you just used. Difficulty climbs from frog to boss-fight.

STATE indigo · TRANSITION rose · BASE teal — the series colors, as always. New paper color so Parts 1, 2, and the Dojo stay separate in your memory: cream → blueprint → sage.

Pedagogy: retrieval practice (attempt before reveal) beats re-reading by a wide margin — that's why answers are physically on the NEXT slide. Coach her to say the 5-step recipe aloud at each Q slide: state sentence, transition, base, order, answer+cost.
REP 1 · dummies Frog Pond
🐸 A frog starts on pad 0 and hops +1 or +2 pads at a time.

Q: How many distinct hop-sequences reach pad 4?

Before advancing, say out loud: the state sentence, the transition, the base cases. (Hint: you have seen this frog before, wearing a staircase.)

The pond, for pondering. Count on your fingers if you want — pad 4 is small enough to brute-force, which is exactly how you sanity-check a base case.

Answer she should produce: state "ways[i] = # hop-sequences reaching pad i"; transition ways[i]=ways[i−1]+ways[i−2] (last hop was +1 or +2); base ways[0]=1, ways[1]=1. It's climbing stairs = fib. The finger-count: pad 4 → 5 ways (11111… no wait, hops: 1111, 112, 121, 211, 22 → 5).
REP 1 · answer It was Fibonacci in a pond
✅ Answer: 5 ways. Every route to pad 4 arrives from pad 3 (3 ways to be there) or pad 2 (2 ways) — and those are ALL the options, so 3 + 2 = 5. The five: 1111 · 112 · 121 · 211 · 22.

Each pad's count = sum of the two pads behind it. Every pad is grown fresh from seeded noise — no two alike, like real ponds and real subproblems.


      
⌨ typed the way you'd narrate it: state → base → transition → answer. Two variables because the dependency window is only two pads wide.

    

  




Narration script to rehearse with the typing: "My state: ways to reach pad i. Last hop was +1 or +2, so I sum the two previous. Bases: one way to be at the start, one way to reach pad 1. I only ever need the last two values, so two variables, O(n) time O(1) space." ~20 seconds, complete.





  
REP 2 · the interviewer edits Same pond, new rules

  
Interviewer: "Nice. Now each pad has a toll 💰 = [0, 2, 1, 4, 1], paid when you land on it. Minimize total toll to reach pad 4 (start is free)."


  Q: What changes in your code — and what stays? Be precise: which lines, which operator?

  
This is the most common interview move in existence: a solved problem, lightly mutated. The skill being tested is knowing which part of the recipe absorbs the change.




Expected answer: the STATE sentence barely changes (ways→min toll), the BASES change meaning (0 cost), and only the TRANSITION's combiner changes: + becomes min(+toll). Counting → optimizing, Part 1's taxonomy. The next slide animates exactly this as a copy-paste-then-edit.





  
REP 2 · answer Copy. Paste. Edit two lines. Narrate.

  
✅ Answer: minimum toll = 2, via route 0→2→4 (pay 1 + 1). What changed: the bases (counts → real starting costs) and the combiner (+ → min). What stayed: the loop, the window, the whole structure.

  

    

      

      
    

    

      
The motion to model

      
Literally say: "I'll reuse my structure" — then change, out loud:


      ① bases: "one way" counts → actual starting costs (0 and toll[1])

      ② combiner: + (count ways) → min(…) + toll (cheapest way)


      Everything else — the loop, the two rolling variables, the window — survives untouched.

    

  




The rose flashes mark exactly the edited tokens. This reuse-and-mutate motion is worth practicing physically: it shows structural understanding AND saves minutes in the real interview. Check: pads tolls [0,2,1,4,1]; min(0→2(1)→4(1))=2 vs 0→1(2)→3(4)... yes 2.





  
REP 3 · two dimensions City Blocks

  
🏠 You're at home (top-left). Work 🏁 is at bottom-right of a 4×6 block grid. You only ever walk east or south. One intersection is closed for a street fair 🚧.


  Q: How many routes to work? First: what's the state, and what does 🚧 do to it?

  
New ingredient vs the frog: the state needs two coordinates now. The 🚧 is testing whether your base cases and transitions survive contact with reality.




Expected: dp[r][c] = # routes reaching (r,c); transition up+left; bases first row/col = 1 UNTIL an obstacle, after which 0s propagate; the obstacle cell itself is 0. This is Unique Paths II (LC 63). Common miss: setting the whole first row to 1 ignoring that an obstacle blocks everything after it.





  
REP 3 · answer The street fair eats a whole lane of routes

  
✅ Answer: 28 routes (it would be 56 with no fair). The 🚧 isn't special-cased — it's simply a state whose value is 0, and the recurrence routes around it on its own.

  

    

      

      
    

    

      
What the animation shows

      
▸ teal edges: one route each — until the 🚧 would block them

      ▸ 🚧 contributes 0 — it's not skipped, it's a state whose answer is "no routes"

      ▸ every other cell: up + left, same as ever

      ▸ Answer: 28 routes (vs 56 with no fair — one closed corner cost half the city's options)

    

  




Key teaching point: obstacles aren't special-cased with if-statements sprinkled everywhere — they're just states with value 0, and the recurrence handles the rest. "Make the weird thing a normal state with a known value" is a deep DP instinct. Grid is 4 rows × 6 cols, obstacle at row 1, col 3 (0-indexed).





  
REP 4 · choices Heist Street

  
🏠 Five houses hold 💰 = [2, 7, 9, 3, 1]. Rob any subset, but never two adjacent (alarms talk to their neighbors).


  Q: Maximum loot? And — recalling your own pre-test from Part 1 — state the state as a value, not a decision.

  
Trace it by hand first: 5 houses is small. Then check yourself: does your English sentence answer "how much?" (good) or "which ones?" (leaking).




This directly targets her Part-1 pre-test miss ("whether to rob house i"). Correct: dp[i] = max loot from houses 0..i. Transition max(skip=dp[i−1], take=dp[i−2]+v[i]). Hand answer: 2+9+1=12. The Q slide literally cites her error pattern — personalized scaffolding.





  
REP 4 · answer 12 — and watch which doors get kicked

  
✅ Answer: 12 — rob houses 0, 2, 4 (2 + 9 + 1). The state is a VALUE ("max loot so far"), never a yes/no decision — decisions are what the transition compares, not what the state stores.

  

    

      

      
      
Top row: the street. Bottom row: best-so-far — rose frames mark houses in the optimal plan (0, 2, 4 → 2+9+1 = 12).

    

    

      

    

  




Note the state sentence in the typed comment — a VALUE ("max loot"), fixing the pre-test error for good. The backtrace (which houses) is recoverable from the dp values afterwards; in interviews say "if they want the actual houses, I'd store the choice or walk the table backwards" — one sentence, don't implement unless asked.





  
REP 5 · the trap The Loot Bag

  
Your bag holds 8 kg. The vault contains: 🗡 (2kg, value 3) · ⚗️ (3kg, 4) · 💎 (4kg, 5) · 📜 (5kg, 8). One of each.


  Q1: Best haul?

  Q2 (the real exam): you compress the table to one row — which direction does the capacity loop run, and what goes wrong in the other direction?

  
If Q2 feels fuzzy, DO NOT advance yet — re-derive it: when you read dp[w−weight], whose row do you need it to be from?




Q1: capacity 8 best = 📜(5,8)+⚗️(3,4) = 12. Q2: descending, because dp[w−wt] must still hold the PREVIOUS item's value; ascending would let item i be counted twice (= unbounded knapsack by accident). This is the deck's hardest instinct; the stop-sign phrasing is deliberate scaffolding.





  
REP 5 · answer 12 — and the loop runs backwards

  
✅ Answers: best haul = 12 (📜 8 + ⚗️ 4, weight 8 exactly) · the loop runs DESCENDING, because dp[w−wt] must still hold yesterday's row (the previous item's answers); ascending would let an item pay for itself twice.

  

    

      

      
      
Rose cells: where take beat skip. Final cell: 12 (📜 + ⚗️).

    

    

      
# one-row knapsack: dp[w] = best value within capacity w
dp = [0] * 9
for emoji, wt, val in loot:
    for w in range(8, wt-1, -1):   # ← DESCENDING: dp[w-wt] must
        dp[w] = max(dp[w], dp[w-wt] + val)  #   still be YESTERDAY's row
# ascending instead? item re-usable within its own row
# = you silently solved UNBOUNDED knapsack. Classic silent bug.

      
One highlighted character carries the whole exam. Say the "yesterday's row" sentence out loud and the direction is never fuzzy again.

    

  




The -1 is wrapped in the .chg highlight on purpose — THE character of 2-D DP interviews. Coin Change flips it: reuse wanted → ascending. Pair them as a before/after in the playground tonight: knapsack descending, coin change ascending, same skeleton.





  
REP 6 · boss fight The Parade Alignment

  
Two parades march past: 

  Parade A: 🦆 🐱 🦆 🐸 🐱     Parade B: 🐱 🦆 🐸


  Q: What's the longest sequence of animals appearing in BOTH parades, in order (gaps allowed)?


  And the structural question: what are the two indices of your state, and what are the two cases of your transition?

  
No numbers in sight — that's deliberate. If the recipe only works for you on integers, it hasn't become an instinct yet. (It will in about one slide.)




LCS in costume. State: dp[i][j] = LCS length of A's first i animals, B's first j. Transition: last animals match → diag+1; else max(up, left). Answer: 🐱 🦆 🐸, length 3 (A positions 2,3,4). The emoji disguise tests transfer — the real interview skill.





  
REP 6 · answer 🐱🦆🐸 — length 3, found by a table

  
✅ Answer: 3 — the subsequence 🐱🦆🐸 marches in both parades. Match → diagonal + 1; no match → carry max(up, left); empty parade rows = 0.

  

    

      

      
    

    

      
Read the animation

      
▸ teal zeros: empty-parade bases

      ▸ rose cells: the last animals match → diagonal + 1 — watch the matches climb 1 → 2 → 3

      ▸ plain cells: carry the best of up/left


      This is LCS (Part 2, slide 7) wearing feathers. Same table runs diff tools and DNA alignment — mention that and the interviewer knows you see through costumes.

    

  




Trace the rose cells: (A1=🦆,B2=🦆)→1 … the chain ends at dp[5][3]=3 via 🐱(A2? no—) verify: A=🦆🐱🦆🐸🐱, B=🐱🦆🐸. LCS=🐱🦆🐸 (A2,A3,A4). The point to land: six reps, five costumes, ONE recipe. Transfer achieved.
